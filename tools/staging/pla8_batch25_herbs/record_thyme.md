**Scope answer to the task's question 4:** aphids on thyme are reported at **thyme scope** by exactly
two documents, `iastate_ext` (a genuine thyme observation) and `uf_ifas` Pasco (thyme-titled but
boilerplate). They are reported at **herb-general scope** by `clemson_hgic` and `umd_ext`. They are
**absent** from `ucanr_marin_mg`, `psu_ext`, `ncsu_ext` (both pages), `csu_ext`, `tamu_agrilife` and

should be cited first.

**Severity `low` is correct and well supported.** ISU's word is "occasional"; NCSU's genus page says
"no serious insect or disease problems"; UF/IFAS Santa Rosa (Florida panhandle, i.e. a hard test)
says "no serious pests or diseases are normally seen on the plant". Nothing read argues for medium.

On the aromatic-oil deterrence hypothesis the task raised: **no document read attributes thyme's low
pest load to its essential oils.** ISU's only oil statement runs the other way (over-fertilizing
"diminish[es] the concentration of aromatic oils"), and the one oil-and-insects sentence found
anywhere is `psu_ext`'s history note that thyme "was burned to purify rooms or repel insects" --
folklore framing, not a pest-resistance mechanism. **Do not write an oil-deterrence rationale into
the ladder.** (Lemongrass's shipped entry "Generally pest-resistant (aromatic-oil deterrence)" is a
sibling precedent; it is not evidence for thyme.)

**RECORD CLAIMS THAT HOLD**

- "use a mound or raised bed" -- the only raised-bed sentence in any thyme document is `uf_ifas` Pasco's, and it is in the **powdery mildew** paragraph ("grow them in a pot or raised bed with well-draining soil to improve air circulation"), not the root rot paragraph. Cross-applied. Mechanistically sound but unanchored as written.
- "Waterlogged ground, not cold, is the underlying cause" -- see below.

**RECORD CLAIMS THAT ARE WRONG**
- "It is ... tied to wet, poorly drained soil **rather than to cold**" / "**The real cause is wet soil, not cold weather.**" This is an over-correction that a source contradicts on both halves.
  - `umd_ext`: "Excessively wet, **cold** soil can cause Mediterranean herbs such as rosemary, thyme, and lavenders to die over the winter." UMD couples wet **and** cold; it does not oppose them.
  - `iastate_ext` reports cold as a **separate, real** loss mode: "Winter injury can also occur on woody stems, particularly after severe winters", and "winter survival varies among species and cultivars."
  The record turns "the usual cause is wet soil" (true) into "cold is not a cause" (false). Reword to something like: wet, poorly drained soil is the usual cause, and wet plus cold soil over winter is a documented way Mediterranean herbs are lost. Do **not** let a ladder rung tell a reader that cold is not a risk to thyme.

**Type note (not an error):** `type: "fungal"` is retained correctly. *Pythium* and *Phytophthora*
are oomycetes, not fungi, but the dataset enum has no oomycete value and 317 entries use `fungal`,
including blueberry's and raspberry's *Phytophthora* root rots. Consistency wins; no change.

**LADDER-RELEVANT FACTS the record does not carry**
- **There is no cure and no chemical rung.** Nothing read recommends a fungicide for thyme root rot. The ladder for this entry is cultural-only, which is unusual and should be stated rather than padded.
- The one document that gives a real management protocol is `umd_ext` southern blight, and it is only reachable if the entry admits southern blight: remove affected plants and fungal growth during the season; deep-plow to bury sclerotia; **raise soil pH above 6.5**; for disposal "seal the plants in a clear plastic bag, expose them to full summer sun for 4 weeks"; sclerotia "are very resistant to heat and are not easily killed in compost piles." The pH rung is notable -- **it points the same direction as thyme's own preferred pH** (`ucanr_marin_mg`: "pH between 6.5 and 7, as it prefers slightly alkaline conditions"; the record's preferred band is [6.0, 8.0]). A "keep the pH up" rung is both good disease practice and good thyme practice.
- `ncsu_ext` southern blight adds the environmental trigger: "Humid and warm conditions (around 80 - 95°F), as well as moist and **acidic** soils (pH of <7) favor disease caused by *A. rolfsii*."
- Renewal as a rung: `iastate_ext` "periodic replacement of aging plants"; `ucanr_marin_mg` "It will become woody with age and the center starts to die out after a few years. Replace it or propagate cuttings from the old plant." Replacing a declining clump is a legitimate cultural rung the record does not mention.
- Container guidance: `csu_ext` "Thyme grows well in containers, especially indoors. Do not overwater due to root rot susceptibility." `umn_ext`: "Avoid soggy soil. Constant moisture encourages root rot, especially indoors."
- Watering method: `csu_ext` herb-general -- "Diseases are common on many herbs and spread by splashing water, especially onto plant leaves. Therefore, drip irrigation is recommended, as it prevents disease and conserves water. Water plants at the base, not the top of the plant."

### The shared-id question (task item 2) -- ANSWER: DO NOT SHARE AN ID

Matching the taxon rather than the name, across the four batch-25 crops with a rot entry:

| crop | record name | organism the literature attributes | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| thyme | Root and crown rot | *Pythium* spp. only | VCE PMG 2021 p. 4-6 (unadmitted) |
| rosemary | Root and crown rot | *Phytophthora* root rot, *Pythium* root rot, **plus** Botrytis blight and crown gall | VCE PMG 2021 p. 4-6: "Rosemary (Rosmarinus) -- Botrytis blight, crown gall, Phytophthora root rot, Pythium root rot" |
| lavender | Phytophthora root and crown rot | *Phytophthora* spp., **incl. *P. nicotianae*** | `wsu_ext_lavender_prcr` (admitted); VCE: "Lavender (Lavandula) -- Phytophthora root rot, Pythium root rot" |
| sage | Root and stem rot | not named; CSU says only "stem rot ... prevented by not overwatering" | `csu_ext` GardenNotes 731 |
| oregano | Root and stem rot | "stem and root rots as well as **Fusarium wilt**" (winter overwatering) | `csu_ext` GardenNotes 731 |

The only document that adjudicates these five side by side is the VCE ornamental-disease host list,
and **it gives thyme a strictly narrower list than rosemary or lavender** -- one genus, *Pythium*,
with no *Phytophthora* and no foliar disease, where rosemary gets four entries and lavender gets two.
Lavender's dedicated WSU source pins *Phytophthora*, a **different genus** from thyme's.

**Conclusion: the shared name "Root and crown rot" does NOT denote a shared organism.** These crops
must take separate ids. Lavender's must stay `phytophthora-root-and-crown-rot` (its name already
carries the organism and its admitted source names it). Thyme's is a genuine multi-organism umbrella
and should take a generic id. Two ids already exist in canonical that thyme could reuse rather than
mint: `crown-and-root-rot` (parsley, viola) and `root-and-stem-rots` (tomatillo, marigold, borage,
sweet-alyssum, echinacea, sweet-pea). `crown-and-root-rot` matches thyme's name exactly. **Per the
CLAUDE.md pinning rule the id is set once and never re-derived, so this is a decision for the
authoring pass to make deliberately, before any `varieties[].resistance` or `ladder_delta` points
at it.**

## UMBRELLA RULING EVIDENCE

### Q1. What foliar fungal diseases do US extension sources ACTUALLY report on *Thymus* in the garden?

Answer, after reading every document listed above: **exactly one, powdery mildew, and only from a
document that is demonstrably a find-and-replace template.** Nothing else survives.

| candidate disease | binomial | who names it on *Thymus* | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Powdery mildew | none given | `uf_ifas` Pasco blog (thyme-titled) | **Named, but the identical sentence appears in the oregano sibling post with only the crop name changed (Cross-cutting finding A). No binomial. WEAK.** |
| Alternaria leaf spot | -- | UDel bare bullet list (unadmitted); PlantVillage | **REJECT.** See Q1b. |
| Botrytis / gray mold | -- | UDel bare bullet list ("Botrytis Rot", unadmitted) | **REJECT** -- one unadmitted bare bullet, no prose, no binomial, no symptom description, corroborated by nothing. VCE's ornamental host list gives Botrytis blight to **rosemary** and withholds it from thyme. |
| Rust | -- | nobody | **Not reported on thyme by anything read**, though `csu_ext` gives rust to mint, sage and tarragon in the same document. |
| Downy mildew / *Peronospora* | -- | nobody | Not reported. |
| Southern blight (*Agroathelia rolfsii*) | *Agroathelia rolfsii* (= *Sclerotium rolfsii*) | `umd_ext`, photo-captioned on thyme | **Real, admitted, binomial-resolved -- but it is a CROWN and basal-stem disease, not a foliar one.** It belongs to the root-and-crown-rot entry, not here. |

**Q1b -- the Alternaria trap.** PlantVillage (plantvillage.psu.edu) is the only place on the open web
that supplies a binomial for a foliar fungal disease of thyme. It gives:

> "Alternaria blight (*Alternaria brassicicola*)"

***Alternaria brassicicola* is the crucifer / brassica pathogen.** It is not a *Thymus* pathogen; the
binomial belongs to a different plant family entirely. PlantVillage has pasted its generic Alternaria
entry, brassica binomial and all, onto a Lamiaceae host. This is `match-the-taxon-not-the-common-name`
in its purest form. The page also has no author, and cites CABI 2008 plus a Herb Society of America
booklet rather than a pathology record. PlantVillage is additionally **not in the admitted catalog**
(`psu_ext` covers extension.psu.edu, a different publication under a different editorial process).
**REJECT, and note it so the next reviewer does not re-find it and treat it as a lead.**

The UDel bare list ("Alternaria Leaf Spot") is one line on a one-page Master Gardener card with no
prose, no binomial and no symptom text. Given the PlantVillage error above, I will not treat two
unsourced Alternaria mentions as independent corroboration.

**A second trap worth recording:** searching `thyme + Botrytis` returns a wall of hits. Nearly all of
them are studies of **thyme essential oil used as a fungicide against *Botrytis* on other crops**
(apple, tomato). That is thyme as the *control agent*, the exact opposite of thyme as the *host*.
Anyone re-running this hunt will hit the same wall.

### Q2. Umbrella-by-intent, or is there one disease that should be pinned?

**Neither.** It is not a legitimate umbrella and there is no disease to pin. The decisive evidence is
a document that adjudicates thyme against fifteen sibling herbs under one editorial hand:

`csu_ext` CMG GardenNotes #731, "Herb Gardening" -- verified 2026-09-04. This document assigns a
named disease list to herb after herb:

> "Common chervil pests are slugs, caterpillars, and, rarely, powdery mildew."
> "[chives] are susceptible to powdery mildew, downy mildew, rusts, and onion smut."
> "Fusarium wilt, leaf and flower spot, and powdery mildew are problems for Coriandrum."
> "Dill is susceptible to Fusarium root rot, especially in containers."
> "Fennel is susceptible to Cercospora leaf spot and mycoplasma."
> "Horseradish is susceptible to white rust and bacterial leaf spot."
> "Rust is a major disease of mint... Verticillium wilt, a fungal disease, is also of concern."
> "[oregano] Be careful not to overwater during winter, this increases plant susceptibility to stem and root rots as well as Fusarium wilt."
> "Parsley is susceptible to a few diseases as well: Pythium (damping off), Septoria leaf spot, and aster yellows."
> "[rosemary] Avoid overwatering to prevent root rots or bacterial leaf spots."
> "**Sage is susceptible to rust, powdery mildew, stem rot and fungal leaf spots.** These can, for the most part, be prevented by not overwatering."
> "Tarragon is susceptible to rust. Any plants showing rust should be discarded."

and for thyme, the entire disease content is:

> "**Do not overwater due to root rot susceptibility.**"

The same authors who gave *Salvia officinalis* -- thyme's Lamiaceae cousin, in the same document, one
page away -- four named diseases including two foliar ones, gave *Thymus vulgaris* root rot and
nothing else. **That is an informed absence, not an unexamined one.**

It is corroborated independently by:
- `ncsu_ext` genus page: "This genus has no serious insect or disease problems. Although, it may be susceptible to root rot if soils are wet or poorly drained." -- the one exception NCSU carves out is root rot, not a foliar disease.
- `ncsu_ext` species page: "Insects, Diseases, and Other Plant Problems: No significant problems."
- `iastate_ext`: "Most problems result from excessive moisture and poor drainage. **Root rots and other fungal diseases may develop in wet soils.**" ISU's fungal-disease clause is anchored to **wet soil**, not to humid air -- it is a root-zone statement, not a foliar one.
- `uf_ifas` Santa Rosa County, i.e. the Florida panhandle, about as humid a test as the continental US offers: "**Pest resistance: no serious pests or diseases are normally seen on the plant.**"
- VCE PMG 2021 ornamental-disease host list: "Thyme (Thymus) -- Pythium root rot" and nothing else, in a list that gives rosemary "Botrytis blight, crown gall, Phytophthora root rot, Pythium root rot" and basil "Alternaria leaf spot, Fusarium root rot".
- `clemson_hgic` powdery mildew factsheet host list: 23 hosts, **no herbs**.
- `umd_ext` "Herb Problems": the only named diseases are basil downy mildew and southern blight. Thyme appears twice on that page -- in southern blight (a crown disease) and in winter injury (an abiotic wet-cold loss). **Not in any foliar entry.**

So: thyme does not "genuinely get several minor foliar fungi." Six institutions that were looking
for foliar disease on other herbs in the same breath found none worth naming on thyme.

### Q3. Can anything here be anchored to a catalog-admitted T1 source?

The **umbrella framing** can, at hedge strength: `ucanr_marin_mg` "possible fungus diseases in humid
climates" and `psu_ext` "fungal diseases" are both admitted, both thyme-scoped, and both refuse to

inventing it.

**Powdery mildew specifically** can be anchored to `uf_ifas` (admitted, thyme-titled) but the anchor

**Leaf spot and gray mold cannot be anchored at all**: UNSOURCED-NOT-FOUND, and the closest thing to
a binomial anywhere is a wrong-host error.

### Q4. The honest answer, stated plainly

**US extension literature does not report a named foliar fungal disease of consequence on thyme.**

Six T1 institutions -- NCSU (twice), Iowa State, CSU, UF/IFAS Santa Rosa, UMD, Clemson -- describe
thyme's disease profile and none of them names a foliar fungal disease. The only institution that
does (UF/IFAS Pasco, powdery mildew) does so in a paragraph it also published verbatim about oregano.
The two admitted sources the record actually leans on say "possible fungus diseases" and "fungal
diseases" and stop there.

**Recommended disposition: RETIRE the entry as a standalone problem and fold what survives into
neighboring entries.** Specifically:

1. The one genuinely valuable, genuinely thyme-scoped, genuinely sourced sentence in the entry is
   Clemson's variety guidance -- "the taller growing species and those with smooth leaves will
   tolerate heat and humidity better than low growing or wooly types." That is a real
   variety-selection fact and it should not be lost. It belongs on the crop record (or on the
   root-and-crown-rot entry's prevention, since the humid-region risk it addresses is the same one),
   attributed to `clemson_hgic`.
2. The airflow / spacing / water-at-the-base / shear-after-bloom advice already appears on the
   root-and-crown-rot entry and on the pest entries, sourced better there.
3. If Trevor prefers to keep a fourth entry rather than drop to three, the alternative is to
   **narrow it to `Powdery mildew`** with `severity: low`, cite `uf_ifas` (Pasco) and flag it in the
   record's own `open_findings` as template-derived. That is the honest minimum. It also matches
   the sibling batch crops: rosemary and sage both carry a bare `Powdery mildew` entry today, and
   `powdery-mildew` is an existing dataset id used by 31 crops. **Do not keep the current name.**
   "Foliar fungal problems in humid weather" names no organism, no disease and no symptom pattern
   that identifies anything, and a ladder authored against it would have to invent its own subject.
4. **Do not merge it into the root-rot entry as a foliar claim.** ISU's "root rots and other fungal
   diseases may develop in wet soils" is a soil statement; using it to license a leaf-disease claim
   would be reading a document into supporting something it does not say.

This is the same call batch 24 made when it retired chives' aphids entry. Retirement here is the
finding, not a failure to find.

**Precedent note for the batch:** oregano carries an unsourced bare `Rust` and an unsourced
`Botrytis and humid-weather foliar disease`; lavender carries an unsourced bare `Leaf spot`. CSU
GardenNotes #731, which I read in full for this crop, names **rust on mint, sage and tarragon** and
**gives oregano only Fusarium wilt and stem/root rots**. That document is likely to bear on
