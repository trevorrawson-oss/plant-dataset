- Monitoring signal / method: "Check the undersides of leaves for mites, their eggs, and webbing;

- Nitrogen is a named driver for sap-feeders on rosemary specifically: D12.
- Indoor mite driver named by an extension source: "Dense foliage and poor air circulation contribute

  as powdery mildew, which is the coherent way to write both entries.
- General herb-crop constraint: "It is best not to use systemic insect controls on food plants."
  (D18). This governs the conventional rung for every problem on this crop.

## Aphids and whiteflies [pests] -- severity low, type insect

**STATUS: UNSOURCED-FOUND** (both organisms confirmed on rosemary; the BUNDLE is the finding)

**ORGANISM:** umbrella -- two unrelated families, not one problem.
- Aphids: Aphididae, genus/species not resolved for rosemary by any read document (D8 is a
  family-level Pest Note).
- Whiteflies: Aleyrodidae. D7 names *Trialeurodes vaporariorum* (greenhouse whitefly), *Bemisia
  tabaci* (sweetpotato whitefly), *Aleurodicus dugesii*, *Siphoninus phillyreae*, *Trialeurodes
  abutilonea*, *Aleyrodes spiraeoides*, *Dialeurodes citri*, *Tetraleurodes mori*, *Aleuroplatus
  coronata*, *Aleurothrixus floccosus*. None is host-mapped to rosemary. On an indoor
  overwintered rosemary the greenhouse whitefly is the expected species, but **no document I read
  says so** and I am not asserting it.

> "Good air circulation helps prevent these insects on more susceptible plants, such as germander and monarda"
> "once discovered, they can usually be washed away with a spray of water"

**Where the bundle almost certainly came from (task item 3).** UGA B1170 is in rosemary's existing
citation vocabulary and carries a single combined section headed **"Aphids; Whiteflies"** whose whole
management is one water spray. That is the shape of the record's entry, name and all. But B1170's
sentence attributes susceptibility to **germander and monarda, not rosemary**, and B1170's own
rosemary section (quoted in full below) contains **no pest or disease content at all**:
> "Like lovage and bay, rosemary is native to the Mediterranean area and likes sunny growing conditions that mimic that climate. Drought resistant after established, it should be planted in soil with good drainage. Rosemary is an attractive, shrubby perennial that can reach 3 to 5 ft. There are numerous kinds, including a prostrate variety (R. prostratus) useful in the landscape. Rosemary is a strong-tasting herb and should be used with discretion. As the leaves are dense, many people prefer rosemary chopped finely before it is added to a dish. It is particularly delicious in meat dishes and in breads such as Italian foccaccio."

So the bundling is an **herb-general grouping applied to rosemary**, and the rosemary-specific
support for the pair comes from elsewhere (D1, D9, D11). The claim is true; the grouping is
borrowed.

**ONE PROBLEM OR TWO? -- Two. Recommend splitting into `aphids` and `whiteflies`.**
- **Sources treat them as two.** UC IPM (D1) lists them as two separate invertebrates with two
  separate documents. NCSU (D9) and PSU (D11) list them inside a 4-5 pest indoor roll-call
  (aphids, mealybugs, whiteflies, spider mites, thrips) -- that is a CONTEXT grouping, not a problem
  identity, and if it licensed a two-way bundle it would equally license a five-way one. Only UGA
  B1170 pairs exactly these two, and it pairs them for herbs in general, not for rosemary.
- **Management differs materially, and it differs at exactly the rung a least-invasive-first ladder
  has to get right:**
  - Aphids (D8): "Although aphids seldom kill a mature plant, the damage they do and unsightly
    honeydew they generate sometimes warrant control." / "Knock aphid populations off plants by
    shaking the plant or spraying it with a strong stream of water" / "High levels of nitrogen
    fertilizer favor aphid reproduction, so never use more nitrogen than necessary." / "Ants protect
    aphids from natural enemies." / "Insecticidal soaps and oils are the best choices for most
    situations."
  - Whiteflies (D7): "Management of heavy whitefly infestations is difficult. The best strategy is to
    prevent problems from developing in your garden or landscape." / "If you choose to use
    insecticides, insecticidal soaps or oils such as neem oil may reduce but not eliminate
    populations." / "Avoid using other pesticides (other than soaps and oils) to control whiteflies;
    not only do most of them kill natural enemies, whiteflies quickly build up resistance to them,
    and most are not very effective in garden situations."
  - The difference is the top and the bottom of the ladder. Aphids get a real cultural lever
    (nitrogen, ant exclusion) and a soap/oil rung that actually works. Whiteflies get "prevention,"
    an explicitly partial soap/oil rung, and a documented DEAD END on conventional chemistry. A
    single bundled entry cannot carry "soap works" and "soap reduces but does not eliminate, and
    stronger products are worse than useless" at the same time.
- **Existing ids exist for both** (`aphids` 59 crops, `whiteflies` 5 crops), so splitting costs no
  minting. Note that `whiteflies` at 5 crops already contains one bundle of its own (nasturtium
  "Whiteflies and spider mites"), so precedent cuts both ways; the management divergence above is
  the deciding argument, not precedent.
- **Mirror-image note.** Lavender in this batch carries "Whiteflies and aphids." If both crops split,
  the pair becomes `whiteflies` + `aphids` on both, and the mirror disappears. Worth flagging to the
  lavender reviewer: **UC IPM's lavender page (D22) does NOT list aphids at all** -- its
  invertebrates are Leafhoppers, Spider Mites, Spittlebugs, Whiteflies. Lavender's aphid half needs
  its own anchor and cannot borrow rosemary's.

  ("Aphids found on flowers and foliage"); D8 (aphid morphology); D14 (whitefly recognition).
- "most often on stressed or crowded plants" -- D10 ("Aphids are common in rapidly growing, succulent
  plants that are in crowded conditions").
- "and on rosemary brought indoors" -- D9, D11, D14.

**RECORD CLAIMS WITH NO ANCHOR:**
- "avoid overwatering" as an APHID/WHITEFLY prevention. Overwatering is the root-rot lever on this
  crop (D9, D11, D12, D13), not a sap-feeder lever. No read document ties overwatering to aphids or
  whiteflies on rosemary. This is a correct rosemary instruction filed under the wrong problem.
- "Vigorous, unstressed rosemary rarely attracts these pests." D8 in fact says the opposite driver
  for aphids: vigorous, high-nitrogen, succulent growth FAVORS them ("High levels of nitrogen
  fertilizer favor aphid reproduction"), and D10 says aphids are "common in rapidly growing,
  succulent plants." The record's vigor framing is backwards for the aphid half.

**RECORD CLAIMS THAT ARE WRONG:**
- None outright, but see the vigor claim immediately above, which is contradicted for aphids by D8
  and D10.

**LADDER-RELEVANT FACTS the record does not carry:**
- Ant exclusion is a first-class aphid rung: "Ants protect aphids from natural enemies." (D8).
- Nitrogen restraint is a named cultural rung for both halves: D8, plus rosemary-specific D12
  ("Insects that suck plant sap are generally more prevalent in areas where too much nitrogen
  fertilizer has been applied").
- Monitoring cadence exists for aphids: "Check your plants regularly for aphids -- at least twice a
  week when plants are growing rapidly." (D8).
- Whitefly-specific physical rung: "whitefly populations in the early stages of population
  development can be held down by a vigilant program of removing infested leaves or hosing down with
  water sprays." (D7).
- Whitefly conventional-rung REFUSAL, which a least-invasive-first ladder should state explicitly

  quickly build up resistance to them."
- Biological control is the documented top rung for both: aphid natural enemies (lady beetles,
  lacewings, parasitic wasps leaving "mummified" aphids -- D8) and "In many situations, natural
  enemies will provide adequate control of whiteflies" (D7). D18 adds a rosemary-relevant herb-garden
  note: green lacewing "feeds on aphids, thrips, and other annoying soft-bodied insects," drawn by
  dill, yarrow and cilantro flowers.
- Food-crop constraint on the conventional rung: "It is best not to use systemic insect controls on
  food plants." (D18).

## Spittlebugs [pests] -- severity low, type insect

**STATUS: UNSOURCED-FOUND**

**ORGANISM:** *Philaenus spumarius* (L.), the meadow spittlebug / common froghopper -- per D19 (RHS),
which names both the binomial and rosemary as a host in the same document. D5 (UC IPM) covers three
California species -- *Clastoptera lineatocollis*, *Philaenus spumarius*, *Aphrophora permutata* --
and host-maps two of them; only *P. spumarius* fits a Lamiaceae shrub:
> "*Philaenus spumarius* feeds primarily on broadleaved herbaceous plants, but it also occurs on woody, deciduous species. At least 400 hosts are known."
> "*Aphrophora permutata* is one of several *Aphrophora* species that feed on conifers and nearby herbaceous plants."
D5 gives no host statement for *C. lineatocollis*, so I cannot exclude it from a UC IPM reading on
document evidence; what I can say is that no read document places it on rosemary and D19 places
*P. spumarius* there by name.

**ANCHORS:**

`uc_ipm` https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/rosemary/ -- verified 2026-09-04
> Invertebrates: Aphids / Leafhoppers / Spider Mites / **Spittlebugs** / Thrips / Whiteflies

`rhs` https://www.rhs.org.uk/biodiversity/cuckoo-spit-spittlebugs -- verified 2026-09-04

  plant parts and adjustment of environmental conditions ... should help"; X1: products are "not
  curative and need to be applied preventively").

**SHOULD ROSEMARY JOIN THE 32-CROP `powdery-mildew` ID? My reading says NO, or not without a ruling.**
- The dataset's shared `powdery-mildew` id already carries an organism claim in one crop's display
  name: **"Powdery mildew (*Leveillula taurica*, *Erysiphe cichoracearum*)"**. Those are the
  vegetable powdery mildews from D4. Rosemary's is a *Golovinomyces* (species unresolved in the US
  per X1; *G. biocellatus* per non-US journals) -- same family Erysiphaceae, different genus, and not
  the two organisms the id's own name asserts.
- An admitted T1 extension source states the host-specificity principle in plain language, on
  rosemary, unprompted: **"Differing fungi affect different plants, so the same fungus that causes
  powdery mildew on rosemary may not be the same type that causes it on an African violet."** (D14).
  X1 says the same thing formally: "Powdery mildew fungi are mostly host-specific."
- Practical consequence: joining the id would let a future variety `resistance` grade or
  `ladder_delta` written against vegetable powdery mildew hang off rosemary, where it does not apply.
- **Recommendation:** either mint a Lamiaceae-scoped id (e.g. `powdery-mildew-lamiaceae`) shared by
  rosemary + sage + any other batch-25 herb whose PM is documented, or take the shared id
  deliberately with a ruling recorded that the id is a SYMPTOM class, not an organism class. Do not
  let a bot default into `powdery-mildew` from the display name. **Note sage in this batch also
  carries "Powdery mildew" (severity low), so this is at least a two-crop decision inside the batch.**

**RECORD CLAIMS THAT HOLD:**
- "A white powdery coating on foliage" -- D14 ("White coating on the leaves"); D4.
- "crowded plants" -- D14 verbatim; D9 ("Poor circulation").
- "It is most common on rosemary overwintered indoors" -- D14 verbatim; D11.
- "minor compared with root and crown rot" -- D12 ("rosemary resists most diseases, some cases ...
  have been reported") set against D13's "The biggest cause of plant decline and death is
  overwatering." **Severity `low` is well anchored.**
- "Foliar fungi favored by poor air circulation and humidity" -- D9 verbatim in substance; D15.
- "Improve airflow, thin crowded growth, and space plants" -- D11, D12, D14, D15.
- "Remove badly affected foliage" -- D15 ("removal of infected plant parts").
- "space plants, site in full sun" -- spacing anchored; full sun anchored indirectly (D11: outdoor
  full-sun plants are the resistant case).

**RECORD CLAIMS WITH NO ANCHOR:**
- "and in humid regions" -- plausible, but no read document says rosemary powdery mildew is a
  humid-region field problem. Every rosemary-specific anchor I found is an INDOOR / protected-culture
  statement. The record's own `rosemary_pilot_finding_003` already flags the humid-region modeling as
  under-sourced; this sentence inherits that weakness.
- "do not crowd indoor winter plants" as prevention -- anchored (D14), so this one holds; listed here
  only because the record's `prevention_*` and `symptoms_*` say it twice.

**RECORD CLAIMS THAT ARE WRONG:**
- **"favored by damp, still, humid air"** and **"A leaf fungus that likes damp, stuffy air."** The
  word *damp* is the defect. Powdery mildew is the one common foliar fungus that does NOT want wet
  leaves:
  - D4: "All powdery mildew species can germinate and infect in the absence of free water." (D4 adds
    that water on plant surfaces can inhibit spore germination for some species.)
  - X1: "Unlike other fungal foliar pathogens that need prolonged periods of leaf wetness, powdery
    mildew is inhibited by wet leaves. Drier, but humid conditions, with relative humidity levels
    above 75% is required for powdery mildew fungi to infect and produce new spores."
  The correct consumer phrasing is *humid but not wet, with still air* -- humid air, dry leaves.
- **"avoid overhead watering"** (in `prevention_seasoned`). This is the standard advice for
  leaf-wetness diseases and it is being applied to the one disease where the sources say leaf wetness
  is inhibitory. It is not dangerous advice on rosemary (overhead watering is a bad idea here for
  root-rot and, per D18, mineral-spotting reasons), but as a *powdery mildew* prevention it is
  unsupported by, and mildly contradicted by, D4 and X1. Move it to the root-rot entry or drop it.

**LADDER-RELEVANT FACTS the record does not carry:**
- **A numeric humidity target**, which turns a vague "improve airflow" rung into an actionable one:
  "Manage humidity levels and keep below 70%" (X1); infection requires "relative humidity levels
  above 75%" (X1); "Any method that will lower the humidity, or increase air circulation will help"
  (D15). Non-admitted for the numbers.
- **Nitrogen as a driver**, which no other rosemary problem entry carries: "Powdery mildew infection
  is more severe in plants with high nutrition with new growth flushes. Monitor plant nutrition and
  avoid excess nitrogen fertilization." (X1). Note this is the same lever as D12's sap-feeder
  sentence, so one cultural rung serves several problems.
- **Pruning as a distinct rung from spacing:** "You can reduce the incidence of diseases by pruning
  overgrown plants to improve air circulation within the plants." (D12) -- airflow *within* the
  canopy, not just between plants.
- **A seasonal cure that costs nothing**, which is the best consumer line available and is currently
  absent: "It usually disappears when the plants are moved further apart or are taken outside in the
  spring." (D14).
- **Isolation as a rung:** "you may want to isolate the plant" (D14); "Infected rosemary plants
  should be removed to reduce spreading the disease to surrounding plants of the same
  species/plant family." (X1).
- Scouting cadence and where to look: "scout for powdery mildew weekly in the spring and fall. Look
  on both the upper and lower sides of leaves" (X1).
- **Chemistry, correctly qualified, if a conventional rung is written:** "Mostly fungicides labeled
  for use on culinary rosemary include azoxystrobin, copper, biofungicides, and oils. These products
  are not curative and need to be applied preventively or the first sign of disease to be effective."
  (X1, non-admitted). X1's product table lists azoxystrobin (Heritage), potassium bicarbonate
  (MilStop, Kaligreen), copper sulfate pentahydrate (Phyton 35), *Pseudomonas chlororaphis* strain
  AFS009 (Howler), *Bacillus subtilis* strain IAB/BS03 (Aviv), neem oil (Trilogy). D4's generic
  vegetable guidance (sulfur, horticultural/neem oil, never oil within 2 weeks of sulfur, no
  application above 90°F) is the admitted fallback but is host-scoped to vegetables.
- Damage ceiling: severe infection causes leaves to "turn brown, shrivel, and drop from the plant"
  (X1) -- the record says only "white powdery film," which understates the endpoint.
